The second law of thermodynamics, from a box of gas to the end of time

S=k log W

Carved on Ludwig Boltzmann's grave in Vienna. S is entropy. W counts the microscopic arrangements that look identical from outside. The constant k converts that count into joules per kelvin. Boltzmann died in 1906, a few years before experiments settled that the atoms his formula counts are real.

01S = k ln W

Nothing pushes them apart.

These 360 particles start packed into one corner. The rules they follow run the same forward and backward, and no force drives them outward. They spread because there are incomparably more ways to be spread out than to stay in the corner, and their motion wanders through arrangements without preference. It ends up where almost all the arrangements are.

Watch the energy and the work. The energy never changes. The work it could do drains away.

How the entropy is counted: the observer divides the box into cells and records only how many particles sit in each. W = N!/(n1! n2! …) is the number of ways to assign particles to cells that produces those counts. Change the resolution and the number changes, because entropy measures what a description leaves out. At 2×2 the starting state has exactly one arrangement and zero entropy. Velocities carry entropy too: every particle starts at the same speed, and the histogram relaxes to the Maxwell–Boltzmann curve. “Work it could still do” is the free energy above equilibrium, k·T·(Seq − S), in units of the gas's own kT. The odds in the last tile are (¼)360. If the gas tried a fresh arrangement every second, you would wait about 10209 years to see the corner refill by chance. Keep that number in mind for the end of this page.

Scaled up

When the gas has spread, every joule is still there and almost none of it can be used, because no difference is left to drive anything. The heat death of the universe means exactly this at the size of everything. It is the end of differences, with all the energy still accounted for.

Run it backward.

The collisions in these two boxes are computed with integer arithmetic that is exactly reversible. Reverse every velocity and the gas retraces its history bit for bit, back into the left half where it started. The laws permit un-mixing, and box A will prove it.

Box B is a copy of A with one difference. At the instant you reverse time, every particle in B lands a tiny distance off, by the amount you choose below. Try reversing at about 4 seconds. Then reset, let it run for 15 seconds, and try again.

In a gas, small errors grow exponentially. Each collision multiplies them, here by roughly tenfold every half second, which is why the chart climbs in a straight line. Every factor of a thousand in extra precision buys only a second or two more of recoverable past. Precision cannot keep up with time.

Notice what B does once its error catches up: it starts mixing again, entropy rising, even though you reversed it. Its arrow of time turns back around by itself.

Nothing in B was destroyed. The information about where the particles began is still in the box, smeared into correlations among all 150 positions and velocities. Liouville's theorem guarantees that the exact, fine-grained entropy never changes. What rises is the entropy of every description short of perfect, and every observer that can exist holds a description short of perfect.

Scaled up

As far as we know, the basic laws of physics are reversible in the same way, and most physicists now expect that even black holes give back what falls in, scrambled into their radiation. If so, heat death erases nothing. The full record of every star and every life persists, encrypted, with the key spread across the whole universe.

03Q ≥ kT ln 2 per bit

Keeping anything costs heat.

Write something you want to keep. Each character below is stored as 6 bits, and heat jostles every bit, the way thermal noise eventually flips the bits in a real memory chip. Daniel Temkin's programming language Entropy (2010) makes this the rule for code. Data decays as it's used, so a program has only a short window to say anything.

To repair a bit you need a reference copy, and you have to discard the record of each error you fix. Rolf Landauer showed in 1961 that discarding one bit of information releases at least kT ln 2 of heat, about 3 × 10−21 joules at body temperature. Real computers and brains spend thousands to millions of times more. The minimum is never zero.

You can still read the sentence after a fair amount of damage because English is redundant. Shannon estimated that each letter carries only about one bit of real information. Redundancy is how memories survive noise, and repair is where the bill comes due.

A hard drive, a cell, a habit and a self all last this way. Each is a repair loop running on an energy supply, holding a small set of states against the vast set of others. Karl Friston's free-energy principle describes living things in close to these terms, as systems that keep returning to a narrow range of states.

Scaled up

Repair has to be paid for with a gradient, such as something hot beside something cold, or a fuel and its exhaust. At heat death no gradients remain. Every record ever written, anywhere, goes the way of this sentence when you stop repairing it.

04TSun / TEarth ≈ 5,800 K / 255 K ≈ 20

You run on the difference.

Earth radiates back into space almost exactly as much energy as it absorbs from the Sun. If it didn't, it would keep heating up. What changes is the form. Sunlight arrives as relatively few high-energy photons from a 5,800 K surface. Earth sheds the same energy as roughly twenty times as many low-energy infrared photons at about 255 K. The energy out matches the energy in, and it leaves with far more ways to be arranged.

Erwin Schrödinger put it in 1944: “What an organism feeds upon is negative entropy.” Wind, rain, photosynthesis, digestion and the electrical activity you're using to read this sentence all happen inside the gap between those two kinds of light.

Scaled up

Stars are the universe's only large supply of low-entropy light. Star formation winds down over the next one to a hundred trillion years as the gas runs out. The last red dwarfs fade by about a hundred trillion years, and the gap everything lives in closes.

05S = k A / 4ℓP2

Where the entropy is.

Add up the entropy of everything in the observable universe and the stars barely register. Nearly all of it sits in black holes.

Gravity turns the logic of the gas box inside out. A gas left alone spreads. Matter that pulls on itself clumps, and for gravity the clumped state is the one with more arrangements. A black hole is the limit: the most entropy that can fit in a region of space, proportional to the area of its horizon. Collapse the Sun into a black hole and its entropy rises about 1019-fold.

    After the end.

    Heat death is a condition with no scheduled end, and three things about it are still open.

    It may not be final. A finite system that runs forever eventually revisits its own past, the way the entropy in the first box sometimes dips. That box would need about 10209 years to refill its corner by chance. If the far future behaves like a finite thermal system, the same arithmetic applies to everything: in roughly 101050 years a single brain, complete with false memories, could assemble out of the vacuum, and in something like 1010122 years any arrangement at all could briefly recur. Other physicists argue that empty space settles into a perfectly still state in which no such fluctuation ever happens. Nobody knows which is right.

    Matter may outlast the black holes. If protons never decay, the dead stars are still out there when the last black hole goes. Over about 101500 years, quantum tunnelling would slowly turn each one into a ball of iron, and over spans that need a second exponent to write, even those would collapse.

    And dark energy may not hold steady. Every date in the descent assumes it stays constant, which is the standard model of cosmology. Since 2024, galaxy surveys have hinted that it may be weakening. If that holds up, the far end of this story changes.

    ∞Spast < Snow

    Every arrow points away from one improbable fact.

    You remember yesterday and not tomorrow. Causes come before their effects. You age forward. Each of these is the second law at work, and each depends on entropy having been lower in the past. Follow that back and it ends at the Big Bang, which began in a state of astonishingly low entropy. Roger Penrose estimated the odds of a beginning that orderly, chosen at random, at about 1 in 1010123.

    No accepted theory explains it. Philosophers call the assumption the Past Hypothesis. The whole descent you just scrolled through is that one improbable beginning being spent. Everything on this page rests on it, including the fact that you will remember reading this, and not the other way around.
