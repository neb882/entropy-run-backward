Start with what we can reach. Sunlight takes eight minutes to get from the Sun to you.

One light-day

Voyager 1 left Earth in 1977. It is the farthest thing humans have ever made. On 18 November 2026, after 49 years of travel, it reached one light-day from home.

The gap

Keep going. The nearest star is 4.2 light-years away. At Voyager's speed the trip takes about 75,000 years. Everything in between is empty.

You can stop. This galaxy has about two hundred billion of them.

Pulling back

Everything you just scrolled past is closing into a single point.

The Milky Way

Our galaxy: 100 to 400 billion stars in a disk about 100,000 light-years across. The Sun takes 230 million years to go around it once and has made about twenty trips.

The Local Group

Andromeda, the nearest large galaxy, is 2.5 million light-years away. The light you would see from it tonight left before our species existed. Three large galaxies and about fifty small ones share ten million light-years.

Laniakea

Our supercluster: about 100,000 galaxies in streams 520 million light-years long. Light takes about as long to cross it as complex animals have existed on Earth.

The cosmic web

Pull back again and superclusters are threads. Galaxies gather along filaments and walls, around voids tens to hundreds of millions of light-years wide.

Everything in view

The observable universe: a sphere 93 billion light-years across, holding somewhere between 200 billion and 2 trillion galaxies. There is more beyond the edge. Nobody knows how much.

    In about a billion years

    The Sun, slowly brightening, has boiled away Earth's oceans.

    In about 8 billion years

    The Sun has swollen into a red giant, shed its outer layers, and shrunk to a white dwarf the size of Earth.

    Tens of billions of years

    The Milky Way and Andromeda have roughly even odds of merging within ten billion years. Given longer, the galaxies of our Local Group are expected to settle into one.

    About 100 billion years

    The accelerating expansion has carried every other galaxy out of sight. An astronomer born now would see one island of stars in an empty, apparently static universe, with no evidence left that there was ever a Big Bang.

    About a trillion years

    The afterglow of the Big Bang has been stretched until it is colder than the faint glow of the cosmic horizon itself. The gas that makes new stars is running out.

    A few trillion years

    Sun-like stars are long gone. The light now comes from red dwarfs, small stars that burn so slowly they last for trillions of years.

    Tens of trillions of years

    The red dwarfs are going out, one at a time.

    About 100 trillion years

    The last star goes out.

    1014.5 years

    What remains of the galaxy: brown dwarfs, cooling white dwarfs, neutron stars and black holes. Now and then two brown dwarfs collide and light one small star. About a hundred shine at a time, where there were once hundreds of billions.

    1015 years

    Close passes between dead stars have pulled nearly every planet from its orbit. The white dwarfs have cooled into black dwarfs, too cold to see.

    You are now a thousand times farther from the last star than the last star was from the Big Bang.

    1019 to 1020 years

    The galaxy evaporates. Chance encounters fling most of the dead stars out into intergalactic space. The rest sink toward the black hole at the centre.

    1021 years

    Each ejected remnant drifts off beyond every other one's horizon. From here on, almost everything that happens, happens alone.

    The pace has changed. Each screen now covers four factors of ten. The whole age of starlight is one ten-billionth of the time elapsed.

    1030 years

    The last of the dead stars that stayed have spiralled into the central black hole. It is the only thing left here.

    1034 years

    Here knowledge forks. Experiments have watched for a decaying proton for decades and never seen one, which means protons last longer than about 1034 years if they decay at all. Many theories say they do. Choose a branch with the switch marked Unknown physics.

    1037 years

    If protons decay, dead stars shine again, faintly. A white dwarf lit by its own dissolving protons gives off about 400 watts, a few light bulbs' worth.

    1037 years

    If protons last, nothing happens to the dead stars. Black dwarfs drift on in the dark, cold spheres of carbon and oxygen, each alone inside its own horizon.

    1040 years

    Ordinary matter is gone. Every atom that ever existed has dissolved into radiation and lighter particles. Atoms turn out to have been a phase.

    1040 years

    Matter is still out there, and will be for a very long time. It no longer does anything.

    1041 years

    The black hole era. Black holes are the last large reservoirs of anything, and they leak. Each one loses mass as faint radiation, slower the bigger it is.

    1044 years

    Could anything still be alive? In 1979 Freeman Dyson argued that a mind could last forever on a finite store of energy, by thinking ever more slowly and sleeping in between.

    1048 years

    Twenty years later Lawrence Krauss and Glenn Starkman answered that an accelerating universe closes that door. The horizon's faint glow sets a temperature nothing can cool below, every erased memory costs heat, and the energy within reach is finite. If thinking is a kind of computing, there is a last thought. Others dispute the argument, and the question is not settled.

    Each factor of ten lasts nine times longer than all the time before it put together.

    Halfway down the ruler. In years, everything above this line adds up to 10−41 of the way to the last black holes.

    No event is known for this stretch.

    1067 to 1070 years

    Somewhere beyond the horizon, the black holes left by dead stars are finishing. Each ends in a flash: its last second releases the energy of about five million megatons of TNT as gamma rays. No light from any of them will ever arrive here.

    Drawn to true scale instead of this ruler, with 10100 years stretched across the whole observable universe, the entire age of starlight would be about a trillion trillion times shorter than the smallest length physics can describe.

    Still the same black hole. Still leaking.

    1087 years

    A black hole with the mass of the one at our galaxy's centre today, four million Suns, would evaporate about now. Ours has swallowed far more than that, so it lasts longer.

    Perhaps 1093 years

    The black hole that was the centre of our galaxy shrinks, heats, and goes. For a moment it is hotter than any star ever was.

    As far as anyone knows, that was the last thing that will ever happen here.

    10100 years

    Far beyond the horizon, the largest black holes of all, tens of billions of Suns, are evaporating. After them, no difference in temperature worth the name is left anywhere.

    The dark era

    A thin scatter of photons, neutrinos, electrons and positrons in an exponentially growing void, all at the temperature of the horizon: about 2 × 10−30 kelvin. The energy is all still here. None of it can do anything.

    The picture has not failed to load. This is what there is to show.

    Heat death

    Every joule that ever existed is still here, all of it at one temperature.

      After 10108 years

      Nothing here is hotter than anything else, so nothing can happen for a reason. Things can still happen by accident.

      10103 years

      The clock needs a second storey now. Every so often a few stray particles arrive at the same place at once: a brief, tiny patch of order, gone as soon as it forms. Small accidents are common. Larger ones are rarer by amounts that are hard to write down.

      If protons never decayed, the dead stars are still out there. By about 101500 years, slow quantum tunnelling has turned each one into a ball of iron.

      Approaching 101050 years

      Wait long enough and the accidents get large. Physicists have worked out roughly how long before empty space, by chance alone, assembles a working human brain, complete with a lifetime of memories that never happened.

      101050 years

      That was one. It lasted a moment, and from the inside it could not have told. If the void really does this, such minds outnumber every observer who ever lived on a planet, and you cannot prove you are not one of them. Most physicists take that as a sign the argument breaks somewhere. Some have shown how empty space might settle into perfect stillness, with no accidents at all. Nobody knows.

      On this clock the whole descent is not a moment. Neither was the wait for the brain.

      10101056 years

      In 2004 Sean Carroll and Jennifer Chen proposed that empty space can do something stranger than build a brain. A small patch of it can, very rarely, start to inflate.

      A new beginning

      Hot, dense, expanding, and improbably orderly: entropy low again, the way ours was 13.8 billion years before you read this.

      Whether our own universe began this way is not known. It is one of the few ideas that even tries to explain why the past was so orderly.

      Notes · the mechanism behind the film

      S=k log W

      Carved on Ludwig Boltzmann's grave in Vienna. S is entropy. W counts the microscopic arrangements that look identical from outside. The constant k converts that count into joules per kelvin. Boltzmann died in 1906, a few years before experiments settled that the atoms his formula counts are real.

      01S = k ln W

      Nothing pushes them apart.

      These 360 particles start packed into one corner. The rules they follow run the same forward and backward, and no force drives them outward. They spread because there are incomparably more ways to be spread out than to stay in the corner, and their motion wanders through arrangements without preference. It ends up where almost all the arrangements are.

      Watch the energy and the work. The energy never changes. The work it could do drains away.

      How the entropy is counted: the observer divides the box into cells and records only how many particles sit in each. W = N!/(n1! n2! …) is the number of ways to assign particles to cells that produces those counts. Change the resolution and the number changes, because entropy measures what a description leaves out. At 2×2 the starting state has exactly one arrangement and zero entropy. Velocities carry entropy too: every particle starts at the same speed, and the histogram relaxes to the Maxwell–Boltzmann curve. “Work it could still do” is the free energy above equilibrium, k·T·(Seq − S), in units of the gas's own kT. The odds in the last tile are (¼)360. If the gas tried a fresh arrangement every second, you would wait about 10209 years to see the corner refill by chance. Keep that number in mind for the end of this page.

      Scaled up

      When the gas has spread, every joule is still there and almost none of it can be used, because no difference is left to drive anything. The heat death of the universe means exactly this at the size of everything. It is the end of differences, with all the energy still accounted for.

      Run it backward.

      The collisions in these two boxes are computed with integer arithmetic that is exactly reversible. Reverse every velocity and the gas retraces its history bit for bit, back into the left half where it started. The laws permit un-mixing, and box A will prove it.

      Box B is a copy of A with one difference. At the instant you reverse time, every particle in B lands a tiny distance off, by the amount you choose below. Try reversing at about 4 seconds. Then reset, let it run for 15 seconds, and try again.

      In a gas, small errors grow exponentially. Each collision multiplies them, here by roughly tenfold every half second, which is why the chart climbs in a straight line. Every factor of a thousand in extra precision buys only a second or two more of recoverable past. Precision cannot keep up with time.

      Notice what B does once its error catches up: it starts mixing again, entropy rising, even though you reversed it. Its arrow of time turns back around by itself.

      Nothing in B was destroyed. The information about where the particles began is still in the box, smeared into correlations among all 150 positions and velocities. Liouville's theorem guarantees that the exact, fine-grained entropy never changes. What rises is the entropy of every description short of perfect, and every observer that can exist holds a description short of perfect.

      Scaled up

      As far as we know, the basic laws of physics are reversible in the same way, and most physicists now expect that even black holes give back what falls in, scrambled into their radiation. If so, heat death erases nothing. The full record of every star and every life persists, encrypted, with the key spread across the whole universe.

      03Q ≥ kT ln 2 per bit

      Keeping anything costs heat.

      Write something you want to keep. Each character below is stored as 6 bits, and heat jostles every bit, the way thermal noise eventually flips the bits in a real memory chip. Daniel Temkin's programming language Entropy (2010) makes this the rule for code. Data decays as it's used, so a program has only a short window to say anything.

      To repair a bit you need a reference copy, and you have to discard the record of each error you fix. Rolf Landauer showed in 1961 that discarding one bit of information releases at least kT ln 2 of heat, about 3 × 10−21 joules at body temperature. Real computers and brains spend thousands to millions of times more. The minimum is never zero.

      You can still read the sentence after a fair amount of damage because English is redundant. Shannon estimated that each letter carries only about one bit of real information. Redundancy is how memories survive noise, and repair is where the bill comes due.

      A hard drive, a cell, a habit and a self all last this way. Each is a repair loop running on an energy supply, holding a small set of states against the vast set of others. Karl Friston's free-energy principle describes living things in close to these terms, as systems that keep returning to a narrow range of states.

      Scaled up

      Repair has to be paid for with a gradient, such as something hot beside something cold, or a fuel and its exhaust. At heat death no gradients remain. Every record ever written, anywhere, goes the way of this sentence when you stop repairing it.

      04TSun / TEarth ≈ 5,800 K / 255 K ≈ 20

      You run on the difference.

      Earth radiates back into space almost exactly as much energy as it absorbs from the Sun. If it didn't, it would keep heating up. What changes is the form. Sunlight arrives as relatively few high-energy photons from a 5,800 K surface. Earth sheds the same energy as roughly twenty times as many low-energy infrared photons at about 255 K. The energy out matches the energy in, and it leaves with far more ways to be arranged.

      Erwin Schrödinger put it in 1944: “What an organism feeds upon is negative entropy.” Wind, rain, photosynthesis, digestion and the electrical activity you're using to read this sentence all happen inside the gap between those two kinds of light.

      Scaled up

      Stars are the universe's only large supply of low-entropy light. Star formation winds down over the next one to a hundred trillion years as the gas runs out. The last red dwarfs fade by about a hundred trillion years, and the gap everything lives in closes.

      05S = k A / 4ℓP2

      Where the entropy is.

      Add up the entropy of everything in the observable universe and the stars barely register. Nearly all of it sits in black holes.

      Gravity turns the logic of the gas box inside out. A gas left alone spreads. Matter that pulls on itself clumps, and for gravity the clumped state is the one with more arrangements. A black hole is the limit: the most entropy that can fit in a region of space, proportional to the area of its horizon. Collapse the Sun into a black hole and its entropy rises about 1019-fold.

      These particles pass through one another, which makes the box quick to run and changes nothing about the odds: each particle spends half its time on the left, so all of them are there together one instant in 2N. With 24 you would be here a long while. With 100, the wait is already far longer than the universe has existed.

      Add particles and the wait runs away from you. For the 360 in the box at the top of this page it is about 10209 years. Nothing in physics puts a wall anywhere on this ladder. If the far future really is a warm bath that lasts forever, then in about 101050 years the vacuum assembles a single brain, complete with the memories of a life, and in about 1010122 years it has tried every arrangement there is.

      That has an uncomfortable consequence. Accidents like that would vastly outnumber ordinary observers, and a fluctuated brain holding your exact memories, including the memory of scrolling down this page, could not tell the difference from inside. Most physicists take this as a sign that something in the argument is wrong. Some have shown how empty space might settle into a state where nothing fluctuates at all. The question is open.

      Two other things could change the ending. If protons never decay, dead stars outlast the black holes and slowly turn to iron over about 101500 years. And every date in the descent assumes dark energy holds steady. Since 2024, galaxy surveys have hinted that it may be weakening.

      ∞Spast < Snow

      Every arrow points away from one improbable fact.

      You remember yesterday and not tomorrow. Causes come before their effects. You age forward. Each of these is the second law at work, and each depends on entropy having been lower in the past. Follow that back and it ends at the Big Bang, which began in a state of astonishingly low entropy. Roger Penrose estimated the odds of a beginning that orderly, chosen at random, at about 1 in 1010123.

      No accepted theory explains it. Philosophers call the assumption the Past Hypothesis. The whole descent you just scrolled through is that one improbable beginning being spent. Everything on this page rests on it, including the fact that you will remember reading this, and not the other way around.
