The descent.

Everything so far has been mechanism. This is where it leads. Below this line, scrolling moves time forward, and the ruler on the right is logarithmic: each mark is ten times later than the one before. The picture is our own neighbourhood of the universe.

It takes a while. Most of it is dark. That is the accurate proportion, and even so it is compressed: at the pace of the first screens, the whole descent would run more than four times as long.

    Now · 13.8 billion years

    Hundreds of billions of stars in this galaxy, and hundreds of billions of other galaxies in view. This is about as bright as it gets.

    In about a billion years

    The Sun, slowly brightening, has boiled away Earth's oceans.

    In about 8 billion years

    The Sun has swollen into a red giant, shed its outer layers, and shrunk to a white dwarf the size of Earth.

    Tens of billions of years

    The Milky Way and Andromeda have roughly even odds of merging within ten billion years. Given longer, the galaxies of our Local Group are expected to settle into one.

    About 100 billion years

    The accelerating expansion has carried every other galaxy out of sight. An astronomer born now would see one island of stars in an empty, apparently static universe, with no evidence left that there was ever a Big Bang.

    About a trillion years

    The afterglow of the Big Bang has been stretched until it is colder than the faint glow of the cosmic horizon itself. The gas that makes new stars is running out.

    A few trillion years

    Sun-like stars are long gone. The light now comes from red dwarfs, small stars that burn so slowly they last for trillions of years.

    Tens of trillions of years

    The red dwarfs are going out, one at a time.

    About 100 trillion years

    The last star goes out.

    1014.5 years

    What remains of the galaxy: brown dwarfs, cooling white dwarfs, neutron stars and black holes. Now and then two brown dwarfs collide and light one small star. About a hundred shine at a time, where there were once hundreds of billions.

    1015 years

    Close passes between dead stars have pulled nearly every planet from its orbit. The white dwarfs have cooled into black dwarfs, too cold to see.

    You are now a thousand times farther from the last star than the last star was from the Big Bang.

    1019 to 1020 years

    The galaxy evaporates. Chance encounters fling most of the dead stars out into intergalactic space. The rest sink toward the black hole at the centre.

    1021 years

    Each ejected remnant drifts off beyond every other one's horizon. From here on, almost everything that happens, happens alone.

    The pace has changed. Each screen now covers four factors of ten. The whole age of starlight is one ten-billionth of the time elapsed.

    1030 years

    The last of the dead stars that stayed have spiralled into the central black hole. It is the only thing left here.

    1034 years

    Here knowledge forks. Experiments have watched for a decaying proton for decades and never seen one, which means protons last longer than about 1034 years if they decay at all. Many theories say they do. Choose a branch with the switch marked Unknown physics.

    1037 years

    If protons decay, dead stars shine again, faintly. A white dwarf lit by its own dissolving protons gives off about 400 watts, a few light bulbs' worth.

    1037 years

    If protons last, nothing happens to the dead stars. Black dwarfs drift on in the dark, cold spheres of carbon and oxygen, each alone inside its own horizon.

    1040 years

    Ordinary matter is gone. Every atom that ever existed has dissolved into radiation and lighter particles. Atoms turn out to have been a phase.

    1040 years

    Matter is still out there, and will be for a very long time. It no longer does anything.

    1041 years

    The black hole era. Black holes are the last large reservoirs of anything, and they leak. Each one loses mass as faint radiation, slower the bigger it is.

    Each factor of ten lasts nine times longer than all the time before it put together.

    Halfway down the ruler. In years, everything above this line adds up to 10−41 of the way to the last black holes.

    No event is known for this stretch.

    1067 to 1070 years

    Somewhere beyond the horizon, the black holes left by dead stars are finishing. Each ends in a flash: its last second releases the energy of about five million megatons of TNT as gamma rays. No light from any of them will ever arrive here.

    Still the same black hole. Still leaking.

    1087 years

    A black hole with the mass of the one at our galaxy's centre today, four million Suns, would evaporate about now. Ours has swallowed far more than that, so it lasts longer.

    Perhaps 1093 years

    The black hole that was the centre of our galaxy shrinks, heats, and goes. For a moment it is hotter than any star ever was.

    As far as anyone knows, that was the last thing that will ever happen here.

    10100 years

    Far beyond the horizon, the largest black holes of all, tens of billions of Suns, are evaporating. After them, no difference in temperature worth the name is left anywhere.

    The dark era

    A thin scatter of photons, neutrinos, electrons and positrons in an exponentially growing void, all at the temperature of the horizon: about 2 × 10−30 kelvin. The energy is all still here. None of it can do anything.
